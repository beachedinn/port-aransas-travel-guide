Alligators in Port Aransas

Where they actually live, why the beach is safe, and what to do if you see one inland

Home

Published on November 11, 2026

One of the most common questions visitors ask before a trip to Mustang Island is whether alligators are something to worry about on the beach. The short answer is no. Alligators are freshwater and brackish-marsh animals. They do not live in the Gulf of Mexico, on the open beach, or in the surf zone. The closest reliable populations are inland, mainly at the Aransas National Wildlife Refuge across the bay and in larger marsh systems further north along the Texas coast.

That said, alligators are part of the broader wildlife picture on the central Texas coast, and understanding where they are and where they are not makes for a more informed trip. Here is what visitors should know.

The American Alligator

The American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis) is the only crocodilian native to Texas. Adult males average 11 to 13 feet in length. Females are smaller, typically 8 to 9 feet. They have dark gray or olive coloring and a broad, rounded snout that distinguishes them from the narrower-snouted American crocodile found in south Florida.

Alligators are cold-blooded and most active during warm months, roughly April through October. They bask at the water's edge during the day and do most of their hunting at dawn and dusk. Their diet includes fish, crabs, birds, and small mammals. U.S. populations have recovered from near-extinction in the mid-20th century and are now stable across Gulf coastal states, including Texas.

Why Alligators Are Not on Mustang Island

Mustang Island is a barrier island surrounded by saltwater. The Gulf side is open ocean. The bay side is saline or high-brackish water. Alligators need freshwater or low-salinity brackish water to survive long-term. The few freshwater pockets in the island's dunes are far too small and temporary to support a population.

You will not encounter alligators on the beach, in the dunes, in the surf, in the Port Aransas ferry channel, or in the freshwater features within communities like Cinnamon Shore. The ferry channel is saltwater, and the animals you are most likely to see there are bottlenose dolphins, brown pelicans, and sea turtles during summer months.

Where Alligators Actually Live

The closest place to Port Aransas where you can reliably see alligators is the Aransas National Wildlife Refuge, about an hour's drive north plus a short ferry crossing. The refuge has a designated alligator viewing area along its auto-tour loop. It is also one of the premier birding destinations in the country, with more than 400 documented bird species and the largest wintering population of whooping cranes in the world from November through March.

A day trip to Aransas NWR is one of the best things to do from Port Aransas, especially for wildlife enthusiasts. The birding and wildlife guide to the refuge covers what to expect, how to get there, and what to bring.

Beyond the refuge, alligators are found in brackish bayous in Calhoun County and in the larger marsh systems of Brazoria, Galveston, and Jefferson counties on the upper Texas coast. These areas are well inland or far north of Port Aransas and not places most beach vacationers visit.

When Alligators Are Most Active

Alligator activity follows temperature. They are most visible from May through September, with May being mating season and the peak inland visibility period. Activity slows in October and November as temperatures cool. From December through February, alligators are largely inactive and spend most of their time submerged or tucked into burrows along the water's edge.

This seasonality applies to inland habitats, not to Mustang Island. Regardless of the month, alligators are not part of the beach experience in Port Aransas. For a month-by-month breakdown of what to expect weather and activity-wise, the best time to visit Port Aransas guide covers it.

Safety Tips If You Encounter an Alligator

If you take a day trip to the Aransas National Wildlife Refuge or explore other inland areas where alligators live, these guidelines from Texas Parks and Wildlife will keep you safe.

Keep your distance. Stay at least 60 feet away from any alligator, roughly the length of a bowling lane. For smaller alligators, 30 feet is the minimum. If an alligator starts moving toward you, back away slowly and give it more room.

Never feed alligators. It is illegal in Texas and is the leading cause of alligators losing their fear of humans. Fed alligators become aggressive and are typically removed and euthanized. No photo or interaction is worth that outcome.

Keep dogs leashed and away from the water's edge. Small dogs are at particular risk in areas where alligators are present. At the wildlife refuge, dogs are not permitted on most trails for this reason.

Avoid swimming in freshwater areas at dawn, dusk, or after dark. Alligators are most active hunters during low-light hours. Stick to designated swimming areas and posted signage.

If chased, run in a straight line. The old advice about zig-zagging is a myth. Alligators are fast in short bursts but tire quickly. Running straight puts distance between you and the animal as efficiently as possible.

Report nuisance alligators. If you see an alligator in a place it should not be, such as a backyard, a parking lot, or a road, do not attempt to move or capture it. Call the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department at 1-512-389-4848 or contact a local game warden.

What About Crocodiles

American crocodiles do not live in Texas. Their range in the United States is limited to the southern tip of Florida, primarily in the Everglades and Florida Keys. There are no crocodiles in the waters around Port Aransas, Mustang Island, or anywhere on the Texas coast.

More Wildlife Around Port Aransas

The wildlife on and around Mustang Island is one of the best parts of a visit. Dolphin watching tours run year-round through the Port Aransas ship channel, and bottlenose dolphins are visible from the ferry and the jetties on most days. Five species of sea turtles nest on the beaches from April through July, and public hatchling releases at Padre Island National Seashore are one of the most memorable wildlife experiences on the coast.
