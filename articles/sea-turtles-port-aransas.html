Sea Turtles in Port Aransas

Species, nesting season, hatchling releases, and what to do if you find one

Published on November 11, 2026

Mustang Island and nearby Padre Island National Seashore are among the most important sea turtle nesting beaches in the United States. The Kemp's ridley, the world's most endangered sea turtle, nests here every spring and summer. A University of Texas Marine Science Institute program called the Amos Rehabilitation Keep (ARK) operates in Port Aransas and handles more stranded sea turtles than any other rehab facility in the country.

If you are visiting Port Aransas during nesting season, there is a real chance you will see turtle tracks on the beach, encounter a nest cordon, or catch a hatchling release at Padre Island. Here is what you need to know.

Five Species on the Texas Coast

Five sea turtle species have been documented in the waters around Port Aransas and Mustang Island. Some are common. Others are rare enough that a sighting should be reported.

Kemp's Ridley

The Kemp's ridley is the star of Texas sea turtle conservation and the species you are most likely to encounter during nesting season. It is the smallest sea turtle, about 24 to 28 inches long and 70 to 100 pounds as an adult. Females nest during the daytime, often in groups called arribadas. Padre Island National Seashore, about 30 minutes south of Port Aransas, is the primary U.S. nesting beach for this species. The Kemp's ridley is federally listed as endangered, and the Texas nesting population is part of a multi-decade binational recovery effort with Mexico, whose largest nesting beach is at Rancho Nuevo.

Green Sea Turtle

Green sea turtles feed on seagrass and algae and are often seen around the jetties and the Aransas Pass ferry channel. Juveniles are common in the area. Adults can weigh several hundred pounds. If you take a dolphin watching tour through the ship channel, there is a chance of spotting one.

Loggerhead

Loggerheads are less common nearshore and mostly seen offshore. They have a large reddish-brown head and powerful jaws built for crushing crabs and whelks. You are unlikely to encounter one on the beach, but they are present in Gulf waters.

Leatherback

The leatherback is the largest sea turtle in the world, with a leathery shell instead of a hard one. They can dive past 4,000 feet. Leatherbacks are rare nearshore around Port Aransas, but sightings do happen and should be reported to the sea turtle hotline.

Hawksbill

The hawksbill is a coral reef specialist with a narrow, curved beak. It is rarely seen in Port Aransas waters, though juveniles sometimes show up at Texas jetty rocks. Any sighting is worth reporting.

Nesting Season: April Through July

Sea turtle nesting and track activity peaks from April through July. During this window, biologists and trained volunteers patrol the beaches daily looking for nests and tracks. Kemp's ridleys often nest in the morning, which is unusual for sea turtles and means visitors walking the beach early may encounter a nesting female.

The first nesters typically appear in March. Hatching runs from late May through August, about 50 to 60 days after eggs are laid. A single female often nests several times in a season, roughly two weeks apart, laying about 100 to 125 eggs per clutch.

The sex of the hatchlings is determined by nest temperature. Cooler sand produces more males. Warmer sand produces more females. This is one of the reasons beach conservation and dune protection matter so much on the Texas coast.

Hatchling Releases at Padre Island

Public hatchling releases at Padre Island National Seashore are one of the most memorable wildlife experiences on the Texas coast. The park collects eggs from nests found on the beach, incubates them in a protected facility, and releases the hatchlings into the Gulf once they emerge.

Release dates are announced on short notice because they depend on when the eggs hatch, which is driven by temperature. The park's sea turtle alert line is 361-949-7163. Releases typically start in mid-June and run through July or August. They draw crowds, so arrive early.

Padre Island National Seashore is about a 30-minute drive south of Port Aransas. It is worth a day trip even outside of release season. The park stretches 70 miles along the coast, and most of it is accessible only by four-wheel-drive vehicle, making it one of the most remote and undeveloped stretches of coastline in the country.

The ARK: Sea Turtle Rehab in Port Aransas

The Amos Rehabilitation Keep, known as the ARK, is a sea turtle rehabilitation facility run by the University of Texas Marine Science Institute in Port Aransas. It handles more stranded sea turtles than any other U.S. rehab facility, treating cold-stunned turtles in winter and injured or sick animals year-round.

Cold-stunning happens when water temperatures drop rapidly, usually during winter cold fronts from November through February. Turtles become lethargic and wash ashore. The ARK takes them in, warms them, and releases them once conditions improve. If you visit during winter and find a cold-stunned turtle on the beach, call the hotline immediately.

What to Do If You Find a Sea Turtle

All sea turtles in U.S. waters are federally protected under the Endangered Species Act. Harassing, touching, or disturbing a sea turtle, its nest, or its eggs is a federal crime. Here is what to do if you encounter one.

Keep your distance. Stay at least 50 feet away. Leash dogs and keep children from approaching.

Do not touch the turtle, the eggs, or the tracks. Turtle tracks lead to a nest, and walking on them can collapse it. If you see tracks, leave them undisturbed.

Call the 24/7 sea turtle hotline: 1-866-TURTLE-5 (1-866-887-8535). This line is run by Padre Island National Seashore and is staffed around the clock.

Note the location. Mile marker, nearest beach access point, or GPS coordinates all help biologists find the site quickly.

Stay nearby if safe. If you can, remain in the area to keep other beachgoers and pets away until a biologist arrives. Do not move the turtle into the water or into shade.

Driving on the Beach During Turtle Season

Port Aransas and Mustang Island allow vehicle access on certain stretches of beach. During nesting season, active nests are roped off and marked with stakes and signs. Drive only in marked lanes between the dunes and the water, slow down near roped areas, and watch for nest markers.

If you are renting a golf cart in Port Aransas, stick to the streets and designated paths. Golf carts are not meant for driving on the open beach, and the sand near the dunes is where nests are most likely to be.

Month by Month

March: First Kemp's ridley nesters begin arriving on the beach. Patrols start.

April through June: Peak nesting season. Daily patrols. Best chance of seeing tracks on early morning beach walks.

July: Nesting winds down. Hatching picks up. Public releases at Padre Island are underway.

August: Late hatching continues. Last releases of the season.

September through October: Mostly quiet. Turtles are offshore.

November through February: Cold-stun risk during winter cold fronts. The ARK is busiest during this period. Report any turtle found on the beach to the hotline.
